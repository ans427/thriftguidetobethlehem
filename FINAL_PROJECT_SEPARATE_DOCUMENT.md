# EES 102 Final Project Supplement

## 1) Topic, Issues, and Significance (Environmental Science + Sustainability)

**Topic:** Fast fashion, textile waste, and sustainable consumer behavior in Bethlehem, PA through a local thrift-guide web application.

This project addresses the environmental and sustainability impacts of fast fashion by connecting users to secondhand stores and translating global environmental science into local action. The central issue is that fast fashion encourages rapid production and disposal cycles that increase greenhouse gas emissions, resource use, and waste generation. Textile production is energy- and water-intensive, and synthetic materials contribute to long-term pollution through microfibers and waste leakage into ecosystems. At end-of-life, large volumes of discarded clothing are landfilled or incinerated, adding additional environmental burdens.

From a sustainability perspective, the project supports circular consumption by extending garment life through reuse. Instead of focusing only on awareness, the app provides tangible behavior-change tools: a map of local thrift options, searchable/filterable store listings, and store-level details. The educational page (“Why Thrifting Matters”) links everyday consumer decisions to larger environmental systems, while the map/store features lower practical barriers to action. In short, this project is designed to reduce friction between environmental knowledge and sustainable behavior by making the lower-impact choice (secondhand shopping) easier, visible, and locally relevant.

## 2) Multiple Perspectives, Decision-Making Factors, and Broader Impacts

This project intentionally considers environmental, economic, political, ethical, and behavioral dimensions of the issue.

Economically, the app frames thrifting as both a sustainability strategy and a cost-saving strategy, recognizing that affordability strongly influences purchase behavior. Politically and institutionally, the project acknowledges that consumer action happens within larger policy systems (waste regulation, producer responsibility, trade flows, and municipal disposal constraints). The solution does not assume policy alone will change behavior; instead, it complements governance by improving local access to practical alternatives.

Ethically, the project engages concerns about labor conditions and environmental justice associated with globalized fast-fashion supply chains and waste export patterns. Behaviorally, the design uses convenience and social proof: users can quickly find nearby stores, filter by preferences, and engage with comments/photos, all of which can increase adoption by reducing time and uncertainty costs.

Regarding strengths and limitations of government forms: national and international institutions can set standards and incentives at scale, but implementation can be slow and uneven. Local governments and communities can act faster but often have limited resources. Therefore, this project uses a “current-world” approach: a feasible community-facing digital tool that can operate now while still aligning with broader policy goals.

Beyond human impacts, the project addresses pressures on ecosystems from resource extraction, emissions, water contamination, and textile waste. By shifting some demand away from new production toward reuse, it aims to reduce environmental harm across the full material lifecycle.
